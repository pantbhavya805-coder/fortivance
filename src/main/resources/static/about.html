Why I chose this idea

Looking at how banks manage their vendors today, I saw two gaps that current solutions leave open. Vendor evidence (contracts and audit reports) is still checked slowly and by hand, and when a vendor has an incident, working out the impact and escalating it takes hours. Every hour lost there gives a cyber attack, and the fraud that often follows it, more time to reach customers. I wanted to close both gaps at the source, the vendor, so a bank can act before its customers are affected. Reading notices and contracts quickly and accurately is exactly the kind of work AI is good at, which made this a meaningful use of Claude rather than a decorative one.

The context: Indian banks have moved their most important services onto vendors, from cloud providers and payment switches to SMS gateways and core-banking service providers. When one of them fails, the bank's customers cannot pay, and the bank, not the vendor, answers to RBI.

The problems Indian banks face today

  • One vendor, hundreds of banks. In July 2024 a ransomware attack on C-Edge Technologies, a technology provider to co-operative and regional rural banks, led NPCI to cut it off from the retail payment systems. Customers of around 300 small banks (reports range from 200 to 300) lost UPI and other digital payments until an independent forensic audit cleared the vendor. None of those banks was attacked directly.
  • The dependency is deep and growing. 93% of Indian financial institutions surveyed by RBI rely partly or substantially on third parties for core cybersecurity work alone. Behind each vendor sit its own subcontractors, which the bank rarely sees.
  • The clock is short. A cyber incident must be reported to RBI within 6 hours of detection, and the vendor's notice often arrives hours into that window. Working out which customer services are affected still means calls and spreadsheets across IT, operations and vendor management.
  • The rules now demand proof, not trust. RBI's 2025 outsourcing Directions require banks to map dependencies on vendors and their key subcontractors and to hold contracts with audit rights and incident-notification terms. That evidence sits in long contracts and audit reports that are reviewed by hand, often once a year.
  • Small banks carry the same duty with far fewer people. A large bank has a third-party risk department; a co-operative bank, NBFC or fintech may have one or two people doing it alongside other work. They were the ones hit hardest in 2024.

Why this approach

Most vendor-risk tools answer "is this vendor risky?" through annual questionnaires. Nobody was answering the questions that matter during an incident: which of our services are down, for how long can they stay down, how much of the reporting window is left, and do our contracts cover this? The work behind those answers is mostly reading (notices, contracts, audit reports) and tracing (vendor to system to service), which is exactly where Claude and ordinary code are each strongest. So Fortivance lets Claude read and draft, lets code do everything that must be exact, and leaves every decision to a person. It is built so that a two-person team at a small bank can do what a large bank's department does.

How Fortivance uses AI responsibly

  • People decideEvery AI output is marked as needing review. Response plans need an approver; evidence findings need an analyst to verify or dispute them.
  • No regulatory verdictsFortivance shows the reporting window but never decides whether an incident is reportable and never files a report.
  • Citations that can be checkedThe AI may cite only clause identifiers from the document. Fortivance checks that each one exists and shows the original text; anything it cannot verify is sent back for review.
  • Untrusted input stays untrustedUploaded notices are treated as data. Instructions hidden inside them are flagged and ignored, and a notice from an unrecognised domain gets no automatic plan.
  • Explainable risk scoresVendor risk scores are rule-based. Each contributing factor and its points are listed.
  • Controlled costResults are cached, every AI call is logged with its token usage, and a daily spending cap is enforced.
  • Measured against gold dataAI outputs for incident extraction, evidence mapping and citations are continuously measured against hand-labelled data on the Evaluation page.

From demo to a real bank

Fortivance does not offer an open sign-up where banks load their vendors into one shared system. Each bank gets its own dedicated instance and loads its register from Excel. That is a deliberate choice, driven by RBI's rules.

Why RBI's rules point to a dedicated instance per bank

For a bank, using Fortivance is itself an IT outsourcing arrangement, covered by the same RBI outsourcing Directions it helps with. The bank must keep full audit and inspection rights for itself and for RBI, keep its data confidential and separated from other clients of the provider, and keep payment data stored in India. A shared platform holding many banks' vendor contracts and incident data would make each of those duties harder to prove, and every bank's own outsourcing review would have to approve it. A dedicated instance, hosted in India or on the bank's own infrastructure, satisfies them directly.

Fortivance already ships that way: one Docker stack (web server, application, database) that starts with a single command, with a data model organised by bank. Onboarding a bank is then a matter of loading its register: vendors and their subcontractors, the bank's systems, and the business services customers use. That works today through the Import Vendor Register page (sign in first; it is under Dependency Graph) and three Excel templates (vendors, systems, services). Files are checked line by line before anything is saved, records are matched by vendor name and system or service code, and nothing is ever deleted.
